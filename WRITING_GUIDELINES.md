# Writing Guidelines — The Book of Salt

## Voice & Tone

The voice of this book is **warm, accessible, and theologically grounded**—written for readers who may or may not have biblical expertise but are seeking spiritual meaning and practical wisdom.

### Characteristics
- **Conversational but intentional** — Write as if speaking to a thoughtful friend, not lecturing
- **Metaphor-driven** — Let the salt metaphor do the heavy lifting; don't overexplain
- **Balanced** — Honor both the folk/spiritual traditions AND the biblical/theological truth
- **Practical over abstract** — Every concept should connect to something a reader can do or understand
- **Humble about mystery** — Acknowledge what we don't know; don't claim false certainty

### Language Choices
- Use "you" and "your" to create intimacy and agency
- Prefer active voice: "Salt preserves meat" not "Meat is preserved by salt"
- Use concrete imagery: "Your presence seasons the room" works better than "You influence spiritual atmosphere"
- Avoid jargon unless defined; if you use theological terms, briefly explain them
- Include sensory details (taste, sight, scent) to make metaphors visceral

---

## Biblical Grounding

Every chapter should have theological integrity.

### Scripture Integration
- **Root claims in specific passages** — Don't say "the Bible teaches..." without pointing to actual verses
- **Provide context** — A verse's meaning shifts with historical and cultural understanding
- **Acknowledge nuance** — Some passages contradict surface readings; that's not a problem, it's interesting
- **Invite readers to verify** — Structure your writing so readers can look up references and deepen their own understanding

### Key Passages to Anchor
- **Matthew 5:13** — "You are the salt of the earth..."
- **Numbers 18:19** — "Covenant of salt" (inheritance)
- **2 Chronicles 13:5** — "Covenant of salt" (kingdom)
- **Leviticus 2:13** — "Season all your grain offerings with salt"
- **Colossians 4:6** — "Let your conversation be always full of grace, seasoned with salt"
- **Mark 9:49-50** — "Everyone will be salted with fire"

## Structure Within Chapters

Each chapter should follow this arc:

### Opening (1-2 pages)
- Start with a concrete image or question that makes readers care
- Introduce the chapter's central idea clearly
- Example: "Folk traditions say salt absorbs negative energy. But Scripture says something stranger..."

### Development (3-8 pages)
- Deepen the idea with biblical evidence, historical context, or metaphorical exploration
- Use subheadings to break up longer sections
- Include 2-3 specific scriptural references with brief explanation
- Bring in cross-cultural or historical perspective where relevant

### Practical Application (2-4 pages)
- Shift from "why" to "so what" — what should the reader do with this understanding?
- Provide 2-3 concrete practices, routines, or reflection prompts
- Give frequency guidance (daily, weekly, monthly, as-needed)
- Include real-world scenarios where applicable

### Closing (1 page)
- Bring threads together with a single clear takeaway
- Gesture toward the next chapter if appropriate
- Leave readers with something they can sit with

## Recurring Elements

### Reflection Prompts
End chapters or sections with 1-2 open-ended questions readers can journal on:
- "Where do you feel 'unsalty' in your life right now?"
- "What would it look like if your presence seasoned this space?"

### Practice Boxes
Set off concrete routines in a visual way:

> **Daily Practice: Salt Your Mind**
> 
> Each morning, before scrolling or speaking:
> 1. Pause for three breaths
> 2. Set an intention: "Let my words bring flavor, not toxins"
> 3. Notice: What thoughts need filtering today?

### Sidebar References
When referencing other chapters or appendices, make it easy:
- "See Chapter 6: Presence as Preservation for more on this"
- "Appendix A has the full list of biblical salt passages"

## Tone Adjustments by Section

### Part I: Foundation
- **Scholarly but warm** — You're teaching history and theology, but not coldly
- **Invite curiosity** — Make readers want to know what salt meant to ancient peoples
- Tone: "Did you know...?"

### Part II: Dynamics
- **Philosophical and metaphorical** — This is where the big idea lives
- **Slightly more abstract** — But always grounded in the concrete (salt itself, specific scenarios)
- Tone: "Here's what changes when you understand..."

### Part III: Practice
- **Encouraging and specific** — Remove barriers to practice
- **Normalize struggle** — "It's normal if this feels awkward at first"
- Tone: "Try this. Here's how..."

### Part IV: Deeper Work
- **Contemplative** — Invite deeper reflection
- **Honest about complexity** — Acknowledge paradoxes and tensions
- Tone: "What if we go deeper..."

## Avoiding Common Pitfalls

### ❌ Don't do this:
- **Spiritualize everything** — Not every problem needs a spiritual practice; sometimes you need sleep or a therapist
- **Overcomplicate metaphors** — "Salt absorbs moisture AND preserves meat AND symbolizes covenant AND..." Stop. Let one image breathe
- **Preach without evidence** — Always ground claims in Scripture or observed reality
- **Write vaguely about practice** — "Practice gratitude daily" is less useful than "Each morning, name three specific things"
- **Assume Christian literacy** — Explain references; don't assume everyone knows the Gospel of Matthew

### ✅ Do this:
- **Be honest** — If a practice didn't work for you, say so. Say what did work instead
- **Use examples** — Concrete scenarios beat abstract principles every time
- **Trust the metaphor** — Salt doesn't need embellishment; it's already powerful
- **Leave space** — Not every reader will take every practice; that's okay
- **Circle back** — Recurring themes strengthen the book; use callbacks to earlier chapters

## Practical Formatting

- Use **bold** for emphasis and key terms on first mention
- Use *italics* for Scripture quotes and foreign language phrases
- Use heading hierarchy consistently (# Part, ## Chapter, ### Section, #### Subsection)
- Keep paragraphs to 3-5 sentences for readability
- Use bullet points for lists, numbered steps for procedures
- Break long sections with subheadings or whitespace

---

## Questions to Ask of Every Draft

Before finalizing a chapter, ask:

1. **Does this chapter make a single clear point?** (Or does it try to do too much?)
2. **Is the biblical grounding clear and specific?** (Could a reader verify these claims?)
3. **Does the practical application connect to the theory?** (Or does it feel tacked on?)
4. **Would someone with no theological background understand this?** (Where do I need to explain more?)
5. **Does this chapter's voice match the others?** (Or does it feel like a different book?)
6. **What's the one thing I want readers to remember?** (Make sure it's clear.)

---

## Revision Checklist

- [ ] Chapter has a clear opening image or question
- [ ] All Scripture references are accurate and cited
- [ ] At least 2-3 specific practices or reflection prompts included
- [ ] Chapter connects backward and forward to other chapters
- [ ] No section feels abstract without a concrete example
- [ ] Tone matches the section (Foundation/Dynamics/Practice/Deeper Work)
- [ ] Closing brings threads together clearly
- [ ] Read aloud: Does it sound conversational, not stiff?
- [ ] No theological claims without biblical or experiential grounding
